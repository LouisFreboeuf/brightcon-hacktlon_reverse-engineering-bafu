This is how we know the method works, and where it stops working. We take ten real unit processes, aggregate them the same way BAFU does, and throw the input list away, then hand the algorithm a candidate input list of deliberately varying quality. The first two rows are what an S1 report feels like: give it the list and the ranges and it recovers sixty-two of sixty-three amounts. The punchline is the bottom two rows. The padded list, where we mixed in ten plausible but wrong processes, reproduces the aggregated flows better than the honest list that was merely incomplete — eighty-eight percent against eighty-seven — while giving a material amount to five inputs that were never in the process. So matching the aggregated flows is necessary but not sufficient, and you cannot use goodness of fit to tell you whether your input list is right. That is the single most important thing we learned. One caveat if anyone asks about the ninety-four percent: the reconstruction with a correct list is exact to machine precision; the missing six percent is about a hundred trace flows sitting at floating-point zero, where a relative ten percent test is meaningless.
