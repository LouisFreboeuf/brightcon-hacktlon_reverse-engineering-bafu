This is how we know the method works, and where it stops working. We take a hundred real unit processes, aggregate them the same way BAFU does, throw the input list away, and hand the algorithm a candidate input list of deliberately varying quality. Every panel is a case where the fit has to set the amounts, which is exactly the S2 and S3 situation; an S1 report needs no fit at all. How to read a panel: every dot is one input of one case, its true amount across and its fitted amount up, so on the diagonal means recovered. The bottom strip holds true inputs the rebuild lost, fitted to zero or never on the list; the left strip holds wrong inputs the fit gave a real amount. The axes span almost thirty orders of magnitude, a unit of plant next to kilograms of material, so a miss of fifty percent still looks like a hit; that is why the count within twenty percent is written under each panel. The two scores: the median share of a case's elementary flows that land within ten percent of the original, and, summed over all cases, how many input amounts land within twenty percent of the true value, counting only inputs that contribute at least one percent to some flow. Given the complete list, every elementary flow lands within ten percent and 787 of 803 amounts land within twenty percent. The sixteen that miss are pairs of inputs that the elementary flows cannot tell apart, such as concrete gravel and unreinforced concrete at the same sorting plant, or zinc coating of pieces and of coils: any split between the two gives the same flows. Ranges on top close most of that gap, 795 of 803. A list that omits its least important inputs still gets ninety-nine percent of the flows, but the remaining amounts shift to cover for the missing ones, 539 of 605. A list padded with ten plausible but wrong processes does no harm in this test: the fit sets all ten to zero. That result is optimistic, and worth saying out loud. Here the true inputs reproduce the target exactly, so a wrong candidate has nothing left to explain. The real originals were computed on older background data, and on real data a wrong candidate can absorb that mismatch; this drift is not tested here. The last row is the important one. With no list at all, every process used thirty times or more on offer and 25 cases run, the fit reproduces ninety-eight percent of the flows within ten percent, with a process it made up: a median of 86 inputs that are not in the real one, and 56 of 122 true amounts right. So a good match of the elementary flows says nothing about whether the inputs are right. The fit's job is the amounts; the inputs come from evidence. If someone asks about the fit itself: every elementary flow is one equation, weighted by its relative error, and each group of flows, land use, water, radioactivity, each emission compartment, counts equally; the problem is badly scaled, a unit of plant next to a kilogram of material, so it is solved with an exact non-negative least-squares method on a rescaled system. An earlier version of the fit did neither well, and read far worse on this table; the numbers here are from the corrected fit, which the real rebuilds use too. A note on the hundred percent: we only score the flows the calculation actually determines. About a hundred and twenty of the eighteen hundred flows per process come out of the sparse solve as round-off, so they carry no information and are excluded and counted separately. Genuine trace emissions like dioxins stay in.
